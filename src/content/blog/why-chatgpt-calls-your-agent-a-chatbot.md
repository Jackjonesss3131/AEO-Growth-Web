## The category renamed itself faster than the web could follow

Something unusual happened to this market. An entire category changed its name in about eighteen months. What shipped as a chatbot, a virtual assistant, or conversational automation now goes to market as an agent.

For most teams that wasn't a rebrand. The product genuinely changed — it stopped answering questions and started executing work, calling tools, and writing to systems that matter.

But that change landed in one place and one quarter. Everything else stayed where it was:

- **The roundups** that listed you among the top chatbot platforms, written before the word existed in its current sense
- **Your G2 and Capterra category**, set when you signed up and almost never revisited
- **Your funding announcements**, describing with perfect accuracy the company you were at the time
- **Third-party comparisons** written by people who have no reason to go back and revise them
- **Your own legacy docs and changelogs**, which nobody deletes

Your site says agent. Somewhere between twenty and fifty sources say something else. The model isn't adjudicating which is correct. It's observing which has weight.

You lose on volume, not on argument. That's the mechanism behind a broader failure I've written about in <a href="/blog/being-cited-isnt-being-understood/" style="color: #2563eb; text-decoration: underline; text-underline-offset: 2px;">Being Cited Isn't Being Understood</a> — this category is just where it's most severe.

If the snapshot on record still says chatbot, that's not a hypothetical — that's the text the models learned from.

## What it costs you on a real shortlist

The buyer isn't asking what an AI agent is. They resolved that a year ago. They're asking something like this:

> *"I need an AI agent that handles support tickets end to end, integrates with Zendesk and Salesforce, for a 40-person team. What are my options?"*

That question is a filter. The model has to decide who belongs on a short list, and it decides using what it understands each company to be. If your record says "chatbot platform," you don't make it — not because you're worse, but because the model believes you solve a different problem.

This isn't a side channel anymore. <a href="https://company.g2.com/news/g2-research-the-answer-economy" style="color: #2563eb; text-decoration: underline; text-underline-offset: 2px;">G2 surveyed 1,076 B2B software buyers</a> in March 2026 and found AI chatbots are now the single biggest influence on which vendors make a shortlist, at 54% — ahead of review sites and vendor websites. More pointed for this category: **69% of buyers chose a different vendor than they'd originally planned** based on what a model told them.

Now put those two things together. The shortlist forms in four seconds, inside a chat window, using a description of your company written before you shipped the product you sell today.

And the company that made the list may not have a better product. It has a better file. It appeared in two 2025 buyer's guides written in the new vocabulary. It updated its directory category on time. An analyst described it as an agent in a report the models ingested.

You had the product. They had the sources.

> **"Isn't this just a branding problem I can out-market?"**
>
> No, and that's the trap. Branding reaches humans who are paying attention to you. This failure happens to buyers who have never heard of you, in a window you're not present for, resolved before anyone visits your site. You can't out-message a conversation you're not in. The only lever is what the model reads when it looks you up.

## "Agent" doesn't mean one thing, and the models know it

Even when you're classified correctly, a second problem starts. The word stretched far enough to cover three genuinely different products:

- A rules-based flow with a model writing the final response
- A system that selects among tools to complete a defined task
- Something that operates unsupervised and writes to production systems

Three products, three price points, three risk profiles. Models blur them constantly, because the market blurs them first.

That cuts both ways. If you're more autonomous than the median, you get grouped with the simple ones and look overpriced. If you're deliberately scoped, you get compared against vendors promising full autonomy and look incomplete.

The way out is to stop leaning on the word and start publishing the boundary:

| What your page says now | What the model needs to classify you |
| --- | --- |
| "AI agent for customer support" | "Resolves refund and shipping tickets end to end, no human approval" |
| "Autonomous workflow automation" | "Executes, then routes to a human for anything touching billing" |
| "Agentic AI platform" | "Writes to Salesforce and Zendesk; read-only on your data warehouse" |

The right-hand column is what gets you into a constrained shortlist. The left-hand column is what gets you into a bucket.

> **"Can't I just use the word 'agent' more often on my site?"**
>
> Keyword density was never the mechanism, and it isn't here. Models build a working picture of your company from sources across the web, weighted by how often and how consistently each one describes you. Repeating a term on the one page you fully control changes almost nothing when thirty pages you don't control say otherwise. More on where those pages actually live in <a href="/blog/what-sources-does-ai-cite/" style="color: #2563eb; text-decoration: underline; text-underline-offset: 2px;">What Sources Does AI Cite</a>.

## A worked example, end to end

**Product:** an agent that resolves Tier 1 support tickets autonomously.

**Who:** a VP of Support at a 200-person ecommerce SaaS, running Zendesk, under pressure to cut ticket volume without adding headcount.

**Case:** their deflection rate plateaued at 30% with their current chatbot and leadership wants a number that moves.

**The question that buyer asks the model:** *"What AI agent can actually resolve Zendesk tickets without a human, not just deflect them?"*

**What the model needs in order to name you:** a page that answers exactly that — the named Zendesk integration, the specific ticket types resolved without approval, the ones escalated and why, and a resolution figure with the conditions attached. Not your homepage. Not "the leading AI agent platform."

**What's blocking it today:** your G2 profile sits in Chatbot Software, and the two 2024 roundups ranking highest for this query list you as a deflection tool. The model has three sources calling you a chatbot and one calling you an agent. It goes with three.

**How you know it worked:** run that exact question across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Google once a month. Either you're named and described as resolving end to end, or you're not — and the gap tells you which source to fix next.

## What to fix, in order

Sequence matters here more than in most AEO work. Publishing new pages on top of a broken record just gives the model more material to file under the wrong category.

**First, find out what the models currently say.** Not whether they know you — what they call you. Ask three things across every engine: what your company does, who the best agents are for your specific niche with the stack and team size your buyers have, and how you differ from the competitor that keeps winning. The first tells you your description, the second whether you're eligible, the third whether your differentiation survives.

**Second, trace the description back to its sources.** When a model calls you a chatbot, ask it where that came from. You'll get a short list of pages deciding your identity. Some are yours and fixable today. Others aren't, and those need outreach, not editing.

**Third, correct your directory categories.** G2, Capterra and the rest keep whatever you selected at signup. These profiles carry disproportionate weight because they're structured, maintained, and trivially parseable — exactly what a model prefers when it needs a clean answer about what something is.

**Fourth, publish the autonomy page.** One page stating plainly: these tasks run without intervention, these run then notify, these require approval first. It does two jobs — models use it to classify you correctly, and buyers stop asking it on calls.

**Fifth, confirm the crawlers can read any of this.** Most AI crawlers don't execute JavaScript, and agent platforms skew heavily toward client-rendered marketing sites. If yours is one, none of the above exists to the systems you're trying to reach. Two minutes with the <a href="/ai-crawler-check/" style="color: #2563eb; text-decoration: underline; text-underline-offset: 2px;">Crawler Check</a> settles it — paste a URL, see exactly what each bot receives.

> **"How long until the description actually changes?"**
>
> Changes to your own pages register soonest in engines that search the web on every query. Changes to how third parties describe you take longer, because they depend on other sites updating and being re-crawled. Anyone naming a date for a specific engine is guessing. What you can control is the order and whether you're measuring.

## Quick answers

**Why does ChatGPT call my AI agent a chatbot?** Because most sources describing your company were written before the category renamed itself, and models weight consistency across sources over any single claim — including yours.

**Does updating my website fix it?** Partly, and it's the necessary first move. But your site is one source against many. The durable fix is correcting the third-party pages and directory categories that carry the old description.

**Which sources matter most for AI agent companies?** Review platform categories, category roundups and buyer's guides from 2024–2025, funding coverage, and analyst mentions. Those are structured, frequently cited, and rarely revised by their authors.

**How do I know if this is costing me deals?** Run your buyer's real decision questions, with their stack and team size attached, across every engine. If you're absent or described as a deflection tool, you're being filtered out before anyone reaches your site.

**Is being cited enough?** No. A citation means a model used your page as a source. A recommendation means it named you as worth evaluating.
