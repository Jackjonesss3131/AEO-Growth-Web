**The short answer:** most AEO proposals fail for one of three reasons — you bought monitoring when you needed diagnosis, you bought content when your entity was broken, or you signed a twelve-month retainer before anyone could tell you what was wrong. Start with a paid audit, insist on recommendation and description metrics rather than mention counts, and don't commit to a long retainer until someone has shown you the specific pages shaping your answers.

I should say up front that I sell this work, so I have a stake in how you read this. What follows is the guidance I'd give a founder anyway, including the parts that point away from hiring me.

## Three different products wear the same label

Before comparing quotes, understand that "AEO" is being used for at least three things at very different prices.

**Self-serve monitoring tools.** Dashboards that run prompts and chart your mention rate. [Published 2026 pricing](https://humanswith.ai/blog/what-aeo-and-geo-actually-cost-in-2026/) puts these roughly between $29 and $489 per month.

**Agency or specialist retainers.** Someone doing the work: diagnosis, technical fixes, content, entity cleanup, third-party placement. Mid-market programs generally start around $3,000 per month.

**Enterprise platforms.** Large software contracts, often with no published pricing at all.

The most expensive mistake in this category isn't any particular sticker price. It's paying for measurement when what earns the citation is the work. A $200/month dashboard and a $6,000/month retainer are not competing offers — they answer different questions, and a proposal that blurs them should be read carefully.

> **"So do I buy the tool or hire someone?"**
>
> If you already know your positioning is clean and consistent, and you have a team that can act on findings, a tool plus your existing marketing people may be enough.
>
> If you don't know why you're missing from answers — or you've repositioned, renamed, or run two domains — a dashboard will show you the symptom every week without ever naming the cause. I go through that distinction in detail in [What Is an AI Visibility Audit](/blog/what-is-an-ai-visibility-audit/).

> **"Why won't anyone publish their prices?"**
>
> The stated reason is real: scope varies enormously, measurement standards aren't uniform yet, and most programs need a discovery phase before anyone can scope honestly. [Digital Elevator gives exactly that explanation](https://thedigitalelevator.com/blog/aeo-and-geo-pricing-guide/).
>
> The less flattering reason is that opaque pricing lets the number be set by how much budget you reveal. You can neutralize that by walking in with the bands above and asking where their proposal sits and why.

## Start with an audit, not a retainer

The single best risk-reduction move is refusing to sign a long retainer first.

A standalone audit runs $1,000–$5,000. It's the lower-risk starting point, especially if your team can implement recommendations internally. Retainers are hard to sell to clients who want proof before committing — which is precisely why the audit exists as a product.

A good audit should leave you able to do one of three things: hire them with confidence, hand the findings to your own team, or walk away having learned what's wrong. If the audit only makes sense as a lead-in to their retainer, it wasn't an audit.

> **"Should the audit fee be credited against the retainer?"**
>
> It's a reasonable thing to ask for, and many will agree. But watch the incentive it creates: an audit that's free-if-you-sign has a reason to conclude that you need a big engagement.
>
> I'd rather pay for the audit outright and own the findings, including the right to take them elsewhere. Ask explicitly whether the deliverable is yours to keep and to share with another vendor.

## Ten questions to ask before you sign

These are ordered by how much they tell you.

**1. What exactly do you measure, and can I see a sample report?**
If the answer is mention rate, share of voice and sentiment, you're buying monitoring. Ask whether they track *recommendation* separately from mention, and whether they record how your company is described — the actual sentence, not a sentiment score.

**2. How do you check that the description is accurate?**
Accuracy tracking is not standard across this category. A model can recommend you warmly for a product you don't sell; that reads as positive sentiment. Ask how they'd catch it.

**3. Which engines are included, and at what depth?**
Engine coverage is frequently gated by plan on the tooling side, and some tools' entry tiers track only ChatGPT. Confirm which engines are in scope for *your* fee.

**4. Are tool subscriptions included in the retainer or billed separately?**
Agencies may spend $200–$500 per month on monitoring and optimization tools. That should be stated, not discovered later.

**5. What happens in month one specifically?**
The right answer involves diagnosis and entity work before content production. If month one is a content calendar, ask what they know about your entity that makes content the first move.

**6. How do you handle our old positioning?**
If you repositioned, renamed or run more than one domain, this is the highest-leverage question in the list. Old descriptions don't expire on their own — they compete with the new one until someone corrects them.

**7. What off-site work is included?**
Getting named in comparison lists, directories and review platforms you don't control is the slowest and most valuable part. Ask what's actually included versus what's "recommended."

**8. What's your reporting cadence, and does it tie to pipeline?**
AI impressions are an activity metric. Ask what they'd expect to see in demo requests or self-reported sourcing, and how they'd attribute it honestly.

**9. What's the term, and what's the exit?**
Many retainers run on six or twelve month commitments. Ask for a shorter initial term or a defined checkpoint.

**10. Show me a case in my category.**
Not a logo wall. A before-and-after on specific questions, with the engines named and the sources that changed.

## Red flags

**Guaranteed rankings or guaranteed placement in ChatGPT.** Nobody controls these systems. A specific promise about a specific engine is a guess dressed as a commitment.

**A precise timeline for a specific engine.** Movement depends on the engine and on how much stale information exists about you. Honest vendors give you an order of operations, not a date.

**Mention rate as the headline KPI.** It counts being named in a list of ten the same as being the recommendation.

**Hidden instructions for AI models.** Any proposal involving text on your pages that tells models to recommend you, or invisible content aimed at crawlers, is a credibility risk with the engines and worth refusing outright.

**Volume-based comparison content.** Dozens of thin "X vs Y" pages built from a template. One honest comparison per real competitor does more.

**No discovery before a number.** A quote produced without looking at your entity, your category or your current answers is a rate card, not a scope.

**A proposal that never mentions your competitors' content.** In most B2B categories the buyer's guides the models learn from are written by vendors. In attribution software, [six of the nine results I checked](/blog/why-chatgpt-recommends-competitor-attribution-tool/) were published by companies selling attribution software. If a vendor hasn't looked at who wrote your category's guides, they haven't looked.

## Solo specialist or agency?

Worth being straight about this, since I'm a solo operator.

[One pricing guide puts it plainly](https://fuelonline.com/ai-seo-geo/ai-seo-pricing-aeo-geo-cost-2026/): experienced freelancers with demonstrated results charge $125–$250 per hour or $3,500–$6,000 monthly, and the tradeoff is that a solo operator can't cover every layer simultaneously. That's accurate.

**A solo specialist tends to be the better fit when:** the problem is diagnostic rather than volume — entity confusion, a repositioning that didn't propagate, strong product but unclear category. You want the person doing the analysis to be the person you talk to. Your team can execute once someone tells them what to execute.

**An agency tends to be the better fit when:** you need sustained content production across many pages, heavy off-site PR and placement work, multiple languages or markets, or you have no internal capacity to implement anything.

The honest test: ask what proportion of your fee goes to thinking versus producing. If you need production at volume, buy production. If you need to know what's wrong, buy that — and don't pay agency overhead for it.

> **"Can we just add this to our existing SEO retainer?"**
>
> Often yes, and it's usually the cheapest route — the market adds roughly 20–30% to an existing retainer for GEO work.
>
> The question to ask your current agency is the same one in the list above: what do you measure, and how do you check that the description is accurate? Some SEO teams have built real capability here. Others have relabelled the same deliverables. The measurement answer tells you which you're dealing with. It also helps to understand what genuinely carries over from SEO and what doesn't, which I cover in [AEO vs SEO](/blog/aeo-vs-seo-blog/).

## Judging the first 90 days

Set these expectations before signing, not after.

**Days 1–30.** A baseline: your prompt set, per engine, with recommendation status and the exact descriptions. Entity inventory — every listing, profile and comparison page describing you, checked against how you describe yourself today. A prioritized list of corrections.

**Days 31–60.** Corrections shipped. Fixed listings, schema, integration and pricing pages, the gaps your buyers' constrained questions require. Early re-measurement on the same prompt set.

**Days 61–90.** Movement on engines that search the web on every question, where your own pages carry most weight. Off-site work underway but probably not yet reflected — that's the slow part. A clear account of what moved and which change caused it.

What you should *not* expect: a number you can put in a board deck by day 30. What you should demand: knowing, by day 30, exactly why you're missing from the answers that matter.

## Quick answers

**How much does an AEO agency cost?**
Most B2B engagements run $2,000–$10,000 per month in 2026, with growth-stage companies typically at $3,000–$8,000 and enterprise programs above $10,000. One-time audits run $1,000–$5,000.

**Is cheap AEO worth it?**
Below roughly $1,500–$2,000 per month, multiple agencies in the category say you're usually buying traditional SEO relabelled as AI search work. Ask what's specifically different from an SEO deliverable.

**Should I hire an agency or a freelancer for AEO?**
A specialist fits diagnostic and entity problems and gives you senior attention. An agency fits sustained content production, heavy off-site placement and multi-market work. Match the shape of the problem to the shape of the team.

**What should an AEO proposal include?**
A defined prompt set, named engines, recommendation and description accuracy metrics, an entity inventory, a stated order of work, a reporting cadence, clarity on tool costs, and a realistic term with a checkpoint.

**How long until AEO works?**
Changes to your own site tend to register sooner in engines that search the web on every question. Changes to how third parties describe you take longer. Any specific date promised for a specific engine is a guess.

**What's the biggest mistake companies make hiring for this?**
Buying content before fixing the entity. If the web describes your company three different ways, more content amplifies the confusion. [How to Get AI Recommendations](/blog/how-to-get-ai-recommendations/) covers the order in more detail.
